## Decision Moments

1. **The new home.** A homeowner moves in and faces a bare yard, builder-grade landscaping, or an overgrown inherited space. They imagine what the yard could be. This is the highest-aspiration, highest-budget entry point. References in reviews: "our first new home," "backyard was dirt."

2. **The specific project.** A homeowner decides to add a patio, fire pit, synthetic grass, or raised planters. They have a defined vision and want someone who can execute it. Reviews frequently describe the contractor's role as "bringing the vision to life."

3. **The before/after inspiration.** Homeowners researching on Houzz, Instagram, or Pinterest develop aesthetic references. The contractor whose portfolio best matches those aesthetic references gets called first.

4. **The referral.** "Many of my friends had used him." Word-of-mouth is the dominant acquisition channel. The website converts the referral visit into a contact; it rarely generates cold inbound on its own.

5. **The maintenance entry.** A homeowner starts with a simpler service (clean-up, mowing, fertilizing) and upgrades to a larger design project as trust builds. The website must serve both the design inquiry and the maintenance inquiry.

6. **The reliability test.** Before committing to a deposit, the homeowner is evaluating whether the contractor will show up, communicate, and follow through. The single most damaging review type in the corpus is the contractor who took a deposit and disappeared.

---

## Top 5 Fears

1. **Taking a deposit and disappearing.** Three of four negative reviews in the corpus describe this exact scenario. "Kept the deposit," "Houdini their way out," "never heard from them." This is the landscaping homeowner's primary fear. It is highly addressable on the website via explicit deposit protection language, contracts, and a clear project start timeline.

2. **Not getting what they imagined.** Landscaping is an aspiration purchase. The gap between what the homeowner imagined and what they receive is the primary source of disappointment even in jobs that are technically adequate. Addressable via detailed proposal process, design mockups, and before/after gallery specificity.

3. **Poor communication during the job.** "Bad communication" appears directly in negative reviews. Homeowners want to know when the crew is coming, what they will do that day, and what to expect next. Addressable via communication SLA language ("We send you a schedule before every crew visit").

4. **Being overcharged or scope-creeping.** "Charge first then explain afterwards" is a direct 1-star complaint. Price transparency and itemized quotes are the antidote. Reviews that mention "mindful of our budget" and "gave us options" in the positive corpus confirm this matters.

5. **Property damage or crew misuse.** Landscaping crews work in the yard, around fencing, near pools, sometimes with children or pets present. The trust threshold for ongoing property access is higher than for a one-time roofing crew. Addressable via crew introduction (named, photographed), liability insurance display, and property care language.
