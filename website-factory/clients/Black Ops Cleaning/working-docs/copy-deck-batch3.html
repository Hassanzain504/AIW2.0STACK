Block 02, the problem

The cleaning contract is a compliance exposure

In a medical practice, whoever cleans your treatment rooms is part of your infection control programme whether their contract says so or not.

That is the part most practice managers discover late. A general janitorial firm can clean an office beautifully and still leave you exposed, because the standards that apply here are not cleaning standards. They are OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, and CDC infection control guidance.

The exposure is not theoretical. OSHA serious violations carry fines of up to $16,550 each in 2026, and willful or repeat violations can exceed $165,000. A failed inspection costs more than several years of a cleaning contract, and the cleaning contract is one of the easiest places for a surveyor to find a finding.

The second problem is scheduling. Practices run to audit calendars and patient lists, not to a cleaning rota. A contractor who cannot work around your treatment room availability will either disturb patients or skip rooms, and both end up in the same place.

Block 04, the technical differentiator

Contact time, and why most disinfection is not disinfection

Every EPA registered disinfectant has a contact time on its label. The surface has to stay visibly wet for that entire time for the product to do what the label claims.

We train and audit against contact time, and where a surface dries before the time is up, it gets reapplied. It is unglamorous, it is the single largest difference between two contracts that look identical on paper, and it is the question we would ask your current contractor.

Block 02, the problem

Spraying is not disinfecting

Every EPA registered disinfectant carries a contact time on its label. The surface has to remain visibly wet for that entire period, or the product has not done what the label says it does.

Depending on the product that is anywhere from thirty seconds to ten minutes. Now picture a cleaner moving through a building at pace: spray, wipe, move on. That surface was wet for two seconds. It was cleaned, and cleaning has real value, but it was not disinfected, and the label on the bottle is the proof.

This is not a rare failure. It is the normal way disinfection is delivered, and it is almost never visible to the person paying for it, because a wiped surface and a disinfected surface look identical.

What we do differently, and it is not exciting

We select the product for the pathogen and the surface, we apply it for the contact time the label requires, and where a surface dries before the time is up we reapply. Our technicians are trained and audited on it, and the protocol is written down so you can read it.

That is the whole differentiator. There is no proprietary method, no exclusive chemical and no special equipment. Just the thing the label already asked for.

Block 03, the electrostatic question

What we will tell you about electrostatic spraying

Electrostatic spraying is genuinely useful, and it is oversold.

It applies disinfectant efficiently across a large area, and it is a good tool for broad surface coverage. Two things are worth knowing before anyone sells it to you as a complete solution.

  • Less product applied can mean less time wet. Efficiency is the selling point, but if the surface does not stay wet for the label's contact time, the efficacy drops with it. EPA labels that permit electrostatic application specify the contact time and the spray distance, and instruct reapplication if the surface dries first.
  • The wrap around effect is weaker than the marketing suggests. EPA research evaluating electrostatic sprayers found limited ability to wrap around surfaces regardless of the charge applied.

CDC guidance does not recommend fogging, fumigation or wide area spraying as a primary method of surface disinfection unless the product label specifically permits it.

So here is our position. We use electrostatic application where it suits the space and the product label permits it, as a supplement to manual cleaning and disinfection of high touch surfaces. We do not sell it as a replacement for either, and we would be careful with a contractor who does.

This block argues against a service the client could upsell. That is exactly why it works on a facility manager who has been pitched electrostatic three times this year. It also sets up the Electrostatic Disinfection page in phase 2 to be honest rather than hype.
